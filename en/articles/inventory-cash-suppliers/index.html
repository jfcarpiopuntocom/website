Inventory and cash · Fictional example

Should you reorder stock or protect cash to pay suppliers?

Short answer: before buying again, compare stock on hand, actual demand, payment dates and expected collections. Inventory has economic value, but it cannot pay a supplier until it turns into cash.

A small example you can recalculate

Imagine a shop holding 80 units, each purchased for USD 12. Its recent sell-through is 20 units per month, at a selling price of USD 20. A USD 700 supplier bill is due, while the shop has only USD 500 cash on hand. These are invented numbers, not a client case.

An invented one-period shop scenario. USD unless otherwise indicated.
IndicatorCalculationMeaning
Stock at purchase cost80 × 12 = USD 960Merchandise, not cash
Simple unit coverage80 ÷ 20 = 4 monthsAssuming unchanged demand; not formal inventory days
Cash available todayUSD 500Ready to meet near-term obligations
Supplier payment dueUSD 700A cash requirement, not an accounting margin
Immediate shortfall, before collections700 − 500 = USD 200Not proof of insolvency; other flows need review
Hypothetical cash-funded reorder20 × 12 = USD 240Adds stock and consumes cash

How a new purchase changes the cash problem

If the shop reorders 20 units and pays immediately, its near-term shortfall rises from USD 200 to USD 440 (700 + 240 − 500), before new collections. Selling one existing unit for USD 20 could bring in USD 20 of cash when promptly collected; that is not USD 20 of profit, and those sales are not guaranteed before the invoice is due.

Three questions before reordering

  • When are payments due? List supplier invoices, wages, rent, taxes and expected collections by actual date. A calendar of cash movements is more useful than one total balance.
  • Which items are actually selling? Check units sold per item and returns. An average four-month coverage can hide shortages of one item and excess stock of another.
  • What is the reorder point? In a simplified illustration, 20 units per 30 days, a 15-day supply lead time and 6 units of reserve imply 20 ÷ 30 × 15 + 6 = 16 units. Actual demand and delivery times may vary.

Do not confuse coverage, turnover and cash

The four-month simple coverage calculation is not the formal days-in-inventory accounting ratio. Standard turnover uses cost of goods sold and average inventory for the same period. Cash planning also needs expenses, taxes, credit terms, seasonality, payment dates and uncertain sales.

What to do next

The choice is not always «buy less». It is to separate the stock you need to avoid shortages from the cash you need to keep operating. Check individual items, cash dates and alternatives before ordering.
